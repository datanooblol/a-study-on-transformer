Reading Training Signals

A model that runs without crashing hasn't told you anything yet. This lesson is about the difference between "it works" and "it's actually learning something useful" — using a real 50-epoch training run (numbers, not a hypothetical) as the running example.

Core concept — applies to any training run, any architecture
Related lessons to read alongside this one
  • Lesson 8 — why a validation split exists in the first place; this lesson is about what to actually do with it once you have one

At a glance this looks fine — the loss number in the leftmost column is dropping the whole time, which is the number most people watch. That's exactly the trap.

Signal 1 — train loss falling while validation stalls or worsens

Read the other five columns instead. Item accuracy never really moves past its epoch-10 level. Age MAE is frozen to two decimal places. Price and sum_insure MAE are both higher at epoch 50 than at epoch 5. Meanwhile train loss fell by more than two-thirds. That specific combination — training loss steadily improving, validation metrics flat or degrading — is the signature of overfitting: the model is getting better at the training set specifically, in ways that don't transfer to customers it hasn't seen.

Illustrative shape of the pattern above (not pixel-exact) — a smooth, steadily improving line next to a noisy, flat one is the shape to learn to recognize on sight.

Signal 2 — a learned weight racing away from the others

The same run's uncertainty-weighted loss was printing its learned task weights every 5 epochs. The age weight alone:

Learned age weight1.261.572.463.805.808.71

Nearly a 7× increase — while age's own validation MAE never moved from ~1.23. This is worth understanding mechanistically, not just noticing: the weight (precision) is learned from how small the training loss for that task is getting, and training loss can keep shrinking from memorization long after real generalization has stopped improving. The mechanism has no way to tell the difference between "genuinely getting better at this task" and "increasingly memorizing this task's training examples" — both look identical from where it's standing.

This link is real and intentional (Lesson 10). But nothing in it checks whether validation performance improved too — a task's weight can climb from pure memorization just as easily as from genuine learning.

⚠ Worth knowing — a real limitation of uncertainty weighting

A task's learned weight climbing steadily is not, by itself, good news. Read it next to that task's validation metric. Weight climbing + validation metric improving = genuine, working automatic balancing. Weight climbing + validation metric flat or worse = that task quietly overfitting, and dragging a growing share of the combined gradient toward memorization as it goes.

Signal 3 — context: is the model even doing better than nothing?

19-21% item accuracy sounds low, but low compared to what? With 8 real items, guessing uniformly at random gets ~12.5%. A naive baseline — the simplest possible non-model answer — is the number every other result has to be compared against, not zero.

TaskNaive baselineWhat it tells you
Classification (next item)Accuracy of guessing the most frequent class, or uniform randomIf the model barely beats this, it hasn't learned the pattern — or the pattern is barely learnable at all (see Signal 4)
Regression (next age)MAE of always predicting the training-set mean, or always predicting "no change from current value"A model that can't beat "predict no change" isn't earning its complexity

Without computing this number first, "21% accuracy" and "1862 price MAE" are both unfalsifiable — they sound bad or fine purely based on intuition, not evidence.

Signal 4 — is the task itself noisy? (a ceiling, not a bug)

Sometimes a model plateauing near a naive baseline isn't underfitting — it's because the data-generating process itself has a low ceiling. Revisit sample_category() from earlier: at a mid-range age, the five category weights land close to 0.6–0.86 — nearly uniform. If the process generating the label is itself close to a coin flip most of the time, no model, however well trained, can push accuracy far above what that randomness allows. This was a deliberate design choice (soft, noisy preference instead of a hard customer archetype, precisely to avoid an unrealistically easy task) — and it may have gone far enough that the task's true ceiling is genuinely close to where the model already sits.

✓ How to tell "ceiling" apart from "underfitting"

Compute the naive baseline directly from the known generating weights, not just from the raw label frequencies. If it lands close to what the trained model achieves, the task is noisy and the model is probably fine. If the naive baseline is clearly beatable and the model isn't beating it, that's a real modeling gap — capacity, hyperparameters, or training length — not an inherent ceiling.

What to actually change because of this

  • Track the best validation checkpoint, not the final epoch. In the run above, epoch 10-20 was already as good as this configuration gets on every metric — everything after was pure overfitting. Training a fixed epoch count and keeping whatever comes out last throws away the actual best model you had.
  • Consider early stopping — once validation metrics stop improving for several checks in a row, more epochs are buying overfitting, not progress.
  • Compute the naive baseline before judging any result — for classification, most-frequent-class or random-guess accuracy; for regression, MAE of predicting the mean or predicting "no change."
  • Watch learned uncertainty weights next to their own task's validation metric, not in isolation — a climbing weight is only good news if validation is climbing with it.

These four are diagnostic principles — knowing what to look for. Glossary

Overfitting
A model improving on training data in ways that don't transfer to unseen data — the classic signature is training loss falling while validation metrics stay flat or worsen.
Naive baseline
The simplest possible non-model answer (random guess, most-frequent-class, predict-the-mean, predict-no-change) — the number every real result should be compared against before being called good or bad.
Ceiling effect
When the process generating the labels is itself noisy or partly random, no model can push accuracy far past whatever that randomness allows — low performance here reflects the data, not a broken model.
Early stopping
Halting training once validation performance stops improving, instead of running a fixed number of epochs regardless of what's actually happening on held-out data.
Checkpoint selection
Saving and keeping the model weights from whichever epoch had the best validation performance, rather than assuming the final epoch is the best one.

Checklist

  • Never judge a training run from the loss curve alone — read every validation metric next to it.
  • Train loss falling + validation flat or worse, at the same time, is overfitting — not a healthy run that just needs patience.
  • A learned task weight climbing is only meaningful next to that task's own validation trend.
  • Always compute a naive baseline before deciding a number is good or bad.
  • Low performance can mean an underfit model, or a genuinely noisy task — the naive baseline is what tells them apart.
